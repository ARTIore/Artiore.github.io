# Deepfakes and AI Payment Fraud

A video call feels like proof. A face, a voice, and a moving mouth are presence. They are not identity, and they are not an approval.

This page combines three lines of the same evidence: the video call, the approval screen, and the idea that AI multiplies fraud. The cases and the reported-loss category are single-source. The multiplier is a hypothesis, not a measurement.

## A reported transfer

In 2024, an Arup employee in Hong Kong transferred approximately HK$200 million → [C115](/investigations/digital-exposure/claims/#C115) — about US$25.6 million — after a video call with synthetic versions of the company's CFO and other staff. The figure is reported, not independently audited here. Single-source.

The deepfake was the delivery. The failure was an approval path that treated the call as authentication. That is a mechanism. It is not a success rate, and it is not evidence that every synthetic call works. Single-source.

## The approval screen

The weak point is the person who decides that a familiar face, a familiar voice, or a plausible help-desk story is authority to move money or to reset access. The same pattern is not limited to one channel. Large crypto incidents are examples of an approval failure, not a reason to treat this as a crypto-only problem, and this page does not add theft totals for them. Single-source.

The 2023 MGM intrusion began with a help-desk call, not a Wi-Fi exploit. The public record supports that help-desk vector and a later US$45 million settlement. A larger impact figure attributed to the company was not fetched in this ledger and is not stated here. Single-source.

Looking for visual artifacts is not a control. Quality varies, and a busy person will not reliably spot a fake. A personal question is weak for the same reason. The control is procedural.

## What was reported, not measured

The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center recorded 22,364 AI-related complaints → [C116](/investigations/digital-exposure/claims/#C116) and about US$893 million in reported losses in 2025, the first year it reported the category. That is a reported-loss figure for a broad category, not an audited total, and not a count of successful deepfakes. Single-source.

"AI is a fraud multiplier" is a hypothesis. No sourced coefficient says AI doubles, triples, or multiplies fraud by any specific factor. A multiplier needs a baseline, a stable definition, and a reporting frame. This category is new, so that baseline is thin. The Arup loss is one case. It does not give an average loss or a success rate.

Claims that a region's cases are mostly AI-assisted, and an unverified crypto-scam estimate, are not used.

## What to do

1. Call back on a number you already have, not one supplied in the request.
2. Require two approvers for a payment change. One person's visual judgment is not enough.
3. Agree a code word before the call, not during it.
4. Slow down urgency. A real executive can wait. A fraudster needs speed.
5. Use a password manager and a passkey. Origin binding beats a phished password. Single-source.
6. Train the procedure, not the ability to spot a fake.
